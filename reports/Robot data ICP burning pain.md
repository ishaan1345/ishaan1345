# Data-ops managers carry robotics' burning pain

As of November 2026, the buyer with the most burning and worst-served pain in robot training data is the **data-collection manager at a funded humanoid or manipulation OEM that already runs its own multi-shift teleoperation**. Examples are 1X, Agility, Foundation, Sunday and Telexistence, the tier just below Figure. Their problem is not getting more data. It is turning paid operator hours into episodes good enough to train on. Agility's own job posting names the metrics this manager answers for: **usable-episode yield, rejection rate, throughput per operator hour and rig uptime**. These buyers already pay for the workaround in headcount, and they pay every shift. Operators earn $36–46 an hour on 12-hour rotations, supervisors $80–115K, managers $120–219K, and a newer layer of data-quality leads $100–200K. The seven named vendors sell supply (clips, hours, labeling seats, managed programs) rather than yield, and they cost several times more per hour than in-house operators. The one buyer that has spoken publicly, Figure, said vendors "couldn't hit the throughput, diversity, or quality bar." The weak leg is the deadline. No source ties collection to a dated training run. The clearest deadlines (GXO's Digit sites starting early 2027, Genesis's LG CNS pilot) sit inside customer deployments, which suggests the burning form of this pain is collecting and validating a small in-domain dataset before a site goes live. So a founder should test the deadline before building anything: interview 15–20 people who hold these job titles, ask for their yield number and for the last collection campaign that slipped, and sell a paid yield audit on a week of their own episodes before writing product code.

## Supply is flooding in, but much of it is unusable

The supply side of robot data has never been more crowded. **Mecka** pays gig operators wearing body sensors and iPhones to record human demonstrations. It has raised a reported $60M ([TechFundingNews](https://techfundingnews.com/mecka-ai-raises-60m-from-sequoia-and-nvidia-to-pay-people-to-teach-robots-how-humans-move/)), with reported talks at about a $500M valuation that sources disagree on ([Runtime Wire](https://runtimewire.com/article/sequoia-mecka-ai-500m-robot-training-data)). **Micro1** says it has about 4,000 "robotics generalists" in 71 countries producing more than 160,000 hours of video a month ([Mezha](https://mezha.net/eng/?p=1798256)). **XDOF** raised $70M in June 2026 ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/06/17/robotic-teleoperation-data-startup-xdof-launches-70m-funding/)), and **Encord** raised $60M in February for physical-AI data infrastructure ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/02/26/physical-ai-data-infrastructure-startup-encord-lands-60m-accelerate-intelligent-robot-drone-development/)). YC has funded more supply companies on top of these: Sensei, which uses sub-$300 demonstration rigs ([YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/Ljf-sensei-robotics-training-data-at-scale)), and Praxis, which records factory workers ([Runtime Wire](https://runtimewire.com/article/startup-spotlight-praxis-robotics-robot-training-data)). Labs are also generating at scale. Generalist reports a pretraining set of **more than 270,000 hours, growing about 10,000 hours a week** ([Generalist](https://generalistai.com/blog/gen-0)).

Conversion, not volume, is where the evidence of pain sits. Micro1's footage yield is reported at **about half usable** ([Runtime Wire](https://runtimewire.com/article/micro1-hiring-10000-robotics-video-trainers)). A robotics data founder gives the same figure for contractor-collected human video ([Prismax](https://www.prismax.ai/blog/not-all-robotics-data-is-created-equal)). Even Figure's in-house pipeline needs "extensive filtering, fraud detection, deduplication, rebalancing, and annotation" ([HowAIWorks](https://howaiworks.ai/figure-index-robot-training-dataset-2026/)). Teleop data has its own failure mode. A May 2026 paper finds that novice operators often produce demonstrations that **complete the task but train poorly**, with inefficient motion, repeated corrections and work near joint limits, and that high-quality demos "remain difficult to obtain in practice" ([arXiv 2605.26349](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.26349)). Practitioners add that datasets from different teams are far less interchangeable than they look, because cameras, calibration, control interfaces and kinematics differ ([Menlo forum](https://forum.menlo.ai/t/physical-ai-data-collection-where-are-todays-biggest-bottlenecks/64)). One founder's postmortem says **no two buyers want the same dataset**, and that a supplier who misses spec can be swapped for "a dozen other suppliers" ([Youmind repost](https://youmind.com/landing/x-viral-articles/robotics-data-broken-business)). Mecka's CEO says the same thing from the supply side: the field lacks "curated, processed, clean data" ([Sequoia podcast](https://sequoiacap.com/podcast/inside-the-robot-data-factory-how-mecka-sensorizes-humans-to-train-robots)).

Read together, these sources show generic hours under price pressure while the scarce asset is a trainable episode that fits a buyer's robot body and spec. One trap is worth naming. Collector fraud (fake, recycled or low-effort footage) is plausible under per-clip gig pay, but none of the research found a documented case at a named vendor. A founder should not build the company around it until interviews confirm it.

## Humanoid OEMs already pay $36–46 an hour for the workaround

The money and headcount sit with OEMs that run their own collection shifts. **Figure** has about eight data-operations openings on the first pages of its careers board, out of 103 total ([Figure Greenhouse](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/figureai/jobs/4698164006)). It lists an AI Data Operations Lead at $175–200K ([startup.jobs](https://startup.jobs/ai-data-operations-manager-figure-2-8772073)) and a Data Quality Manager at $140–200K who will "own quality and throughput" ([Jobera](https://jobera.com/job/figureai-data-quality-manager-46668360/)). Another role stands up new data-ops sites in Mexico City, São Paulo and the UAE ([Built In](https://builtin.com/job/data-growth-associate/8817028)). **1X** pays senior data-collection operators **$36–46 an hour** on day and swing shifts. Those operators annotate with internal tools and file issue logs ([1X Recruitee](https://1x.recruitee.com/o/senior-operator-data-collection-day-shift)). Above them are supervisors at $80–115K on 12-hour rotations ([Khosla board](https://jobs.khoslaventures.com/companies/kind-humanoid/jobs/66721588-supervisor-data-collection)) and managers at $120–150K who own an entire shift ([Khosla board](https://jobs.khoslaventures.com/companies/kind-humanoid/jobs/66721582-manager-data-collection)). In 2024 reporting, more than 50 people had held Tesla's Optimus data-collection role within a year, at $25–48 an hour ([BusinessWorld/Business Insider](https://businessworld.in/article/tesla-seeks-data-collectors-for-optimus-robots-offers-48-per-hour-for-the-job-530192)). Foundation ([Rippling](https://ats.rippling.com/foundation-robotics/jobs/0b7d8908-e4e2-4b0f-9113-4e065f7d9882)), Sunday ([startup.jobs](https://startup.jobs/teleoperations-lead-sunday-ai-7885756)) and Telexistence ([Lever](https://jobs.lever.co/tsmg/fe18b4e7-8b48-412e-a3f0-a1530a30cdf2)) all post teleop operator or lead roles. Most pay figures come from aggregator listings, not the companies' own pages, so treat them as close but unverified.

The clearest single description of the buyer is **Agility's Manager, Data Collection** posting in Fremont, listed at **$168–219K** ([Dice](https://www.dice.com/job-detail/10577df3-4762-4a42-b233-5da991d57a3c)). The role runs teleoperation collection for robot learning and owns station and robot readiness, campaign planning, throughput per operator hour, usable-episode yield, rejection rate and rig uptime. It also owns vendor and contract-labor relationships and **build-versus-buy decisions on outside providers** ([ev.careers](https://ev.careers/jobs/786496716/apply)). That gives you a named budget owner, measured on the exact metric a yield product would move, who already has authority to buy outside help. A quality layer is forming around these teams too. Nimble wants a data-quality and annotation manager ($100–140K) who has selected and managed offshore annotation vendors ([Accel jobs](https://jobs.accel.com/companies/nimble-robotics-2/jobs/91061356-data-quality-annotation-manager)), and Mind Robotics is hiring QA reviewers ([a16z jobs](https://jobs.a16z.com/jobs/mind-robotics/fedfb4f0-42b7-43e1-a83a-01f2c53eda75--qa-reviewer-data-annotation)).

These buyers pay in headcount rather than vendor invoices for a simple reason. One data-collection vendor puts fully loaded outsourced teleop at **$118–200 an hour** ([Robotics Center](https://roboticscenter.ai/blog/robot-data-collection-cost)), against in-house operator pay of $25–46. The vendor figure is commercially motivated, but the gap is wide enough that the direction holds. The in-house shift model is the workaround, and running it is the pain. **Figure is the proof of pain but not the customer.** It built its own Index collection app after vendors fell short. It claims more than 16 million uploaded videos ([Alphasignal](https://alphasignal.ai/news/figure-s-index-app-pays-44-000-people-to-train-its-humanoid-robots)), $15M paid to creators ([HowAIWorks](https://howaiworks.ai/figure-index-robot-training-dataset-2026/)) and a $1B+ data and compute commitment. TechCrunch's analysis argues humanoid makers will want to own their pipelines if proprietary data is the moat ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=3163323)). The ICP is therefore the tier that is big enough to run shifts but too small to fund a Figure-scale build.

Across the five buyer segments in the research, scored against the four burning-pain tests, only this tier passes three of the four:

| Segment | Spend or headcount on a workaround | Recurs | Tied to a deadline | Vendors serve it badly | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Humanoid and manipulation OEMs with in-house shift collection (1X, Agility, Foundation, Sunday, Telexistence) | Strong: hourly operators, supervisors, managers, QA leads | Strong: multi-shift, every campaign | Weak: site expansion and "scaling" only | Medium: Figure's statement and a several-fold cost gap | **Primary ICP** |
| Post-training and deployment teams at foundation-model companies in customer pilots (Genesis, Dyna, Skild) | Medium: eval operators, eval-pipeline engineers | Strong (per site and per robot body, inferred) | Medium: pilot acceptance | Unproven: no vendor sells site validation | **Pivot target** |
| Seed and Series A teams on LeRobot-class stacks (Mind, Watney) | Medium: $25–30/hr operators, home-built rigs | Partial | None found | Plausible: vendors want large volume commitments | Tooling beachhead, not burning |
| Warehouse and delivery operators (GXO, Symbotic, Serve) | Strong, but spent on live exception handling, not training data | Strong | Strong: site go-lives | Anecdotal | Adjacent; different pain |
| Academic labs | None beyond grad-student labor | Yes | Unsourced | Free tools compete | Reject |

The other rows fail on specific tests. Seed teams do hire operators at $25–30 an hour ([Handshake](https://app.joinhandshake.com/jobs/11259385/share_preview)) and fight rig failures, such as SO-100 arms disconnecting 5–20 seconds into a recording ([LeRobot #1675](https://github.com/huggingface/lerobot/issues/1675)). But no source ties their spend to a demo or a fundraise. Academic labs fill shared-infrastructure gaps with free tools such as RoboDojo's remote real-robot benchmark ([arXiv 2607.04434](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04434)). Warehouse operators pay heavily for fleet reliability, with roles such as Tutor's $110–160K fleet reliability lead ([Ladders](https://www.theladders.com/job/fleet-reliability-manager-tutor-intelligence-watertown-ma_87317896)) and Symbotic's $62–86K robot teleoperations specialists ([Symbotic](https://symbotic.com/careers/open-positions/R3440)). That spend goes to live exceptions, not training data, and GXO's CEO says humanoid ROI is still "a couple of years away" ([Tech Jacks](https://techjacksolutions.com/?p=103765)).

## Seven vendors sell hours and seats, not yield

The named vendors fail this ICP for a structural reason. Each one sells an input priced on volume. The ICP's problem is converting its own operators' hours into trainable episodes.

| Vendor | What the evidence shows it sells | Why it misses the ICP |
|---|---|---|
| Scale AI | "White glove" physical-AI collection and annotation, sold through a sales inbox ([Scale](https://scale.com/blog/physical-ai)); third-party reviews say it fits large AV programs best ([Avala](https://www.avala.ai/compare/scale-ai)) | Built for large outsourced programs; faces several worker suits plus October 2026 allegations that it surveilled trainers ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/27/why-should-big-tech-know-what-youre-working-on-scale-ai-accused-of-spying-on-ai-trainers)) |
| XDOF | Teleop data pipelines, the GELLO low-cost arm and the ABC-130K dataset ([Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/news/135015-xdof-raises-70m-to-build-the-data-pipelines-for-training-robots/)); some labs already pay it ([Robot Today](https://robottoday.com/industry-briefing/collecting-robot-training-data-is-dirty-unglamorous-work-some-ai-labs-are-already-paying-xdof-to-do-it/6129)) | Sells collection capacity and data, not quality on a customer's own rigs; no public pricing |
| Mecka AI | Human-demonstration data for pretraining; calls enterprise post-training and deployment services a future segment ([Sequoia podcast](https://sequoiacap.com/podcast/inside-the-robot-data-factory-how-mecka-sensorizes-humans-to-train-robots)) | Human-body data at pretraining scale, not the OEM's own robot episodes; is itself hiring a Lead, Data Quality ([Ladders](https://www.theladders.com/job/human-data-architect-quality-mecka-ai-new-york-ny_87155009)) |
| Micro1 | Crowd video capture at scale, priced on weekly volume guarantees ([Micro1 job board](https://jobs.micro1.ai/post/814abe36-2808-469e-b362-539acf2b544f)) | Requires volume commitments; about half its footage is reported usable |
| Mercor | No robot-specific offering found; misclassification suit alleging 30,000+ workers ([Law360](https://www.law360.com/tax-authority/articles/2475907)) | No evidence it fits robot data; carries labor and breach litigation |
| Encord | Data platform, starts with pilots ([Encord](https://encord.com/blog/best-data-collection-services/)); its robotics collection push is still partly a trial ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/26/are-brain-waves-the-next-unlock-for-physical-ai/)) | Built for teams that already hold data; curates after capture rather than fixing yield at the point of collection |
| Labelbox | Labeling platform; hiring a robotics data pipeline and QA engineer to build automated QA with heuristics, statistics and LLM validation ([Labelbox](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/labelbox/jobs/5003648007)) | Moving toward the gap now, which makes it the closest future competitor |

Labor exposure is a second, subtler failure. Scale and Mercor carry misclassification, layoff and breach litigation ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/22/scale-ai-is-facing-a-third-worker-lawsuit-in-about-a-month)). Crowd collection in India reportedly pays factory workers about 250 rupees an hour, with consent obtained through factory management rather than from the workers ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/amp/story/world%2Fasia%2Findia%2Ffrom-kitchens-to-factories-indians-train-future-robots-1.500571023); [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/04/01/1134863/)). No buyer-side lawsuit was found. Still, an OEM that keeps collection on W-2 payroll avoids this exposure, and even vendor Welo now advertises W-2 teleop roles at $35 an hour ([career.com](https://www.career.com/job/welo-data/humanoid-robot-teleoperator/j202605091023543861691)). The case that outsourced teleop staffing is widely bought is also weaker than it looks. The "TSMG" teleoperator postings that one researcher read as a third-party labor vendor sit on the same Lever account as Telexistence's own teleop leads.

The biggest caveat in this section: **no named buyer complaint against any of the seven vendors surfaced**. "Serves it badly" rests on Figure's self-reported statement, on the cost gap, and on the mismatch between selling volume and needing yield. Mecka's QA-lead hire and Labelbox's automated-QA hire show the vendors know where the gap is and are already staffing toward it.

## The missing deadline lives at the customer site

The deadline is the weakest test across every segment researched. For in-house data ops, the only deadline evidence is expansion: Figure standing up sites abroad and a manager role meant to run collection "for scaling Helix" ([zerogtalent](https://zerogtalent.com/robotics-jobs/figure-ai/helix-data-creator-4585080006)). No training-run date appears anywhere. Generalist's roughly five-month gap between GEN-0 and GEN-1 implies recurring release pressure ([The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/generalist-introduces-gen-1-general-purpose-model-for-physical-ai/)), but nothing ties a release to a data purchase.

Hard dates do appear once robots go to customers. Agility and GXO announced on October 26, 2026 that Digit will deploy at two new GXO sites in Statesville, North Carolina and Lebanon, Tennessee **starting early 2027** ([The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/agility-gxo-logistics-plan-expanded-deployment-digit-humanoid/)). Genesis's June 2026 pilot with LG CNS uses a playbook that covers workflow documentation, **data collection, post-training and validation** for a customer deployment ([Genesis](https://www.genesis.ai/press/partnering-with-lg-cns)). Symbotic's next GreenBox site was guided "within 60 to 90 days" ([Nasdaq](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/symbotic-q3-earnings-call-highlights)). At the same time, the in-domain share of the data recipe is shrinking and becoming more decisive. Mecka's CEO describes a mix converging on about a million hours of human data, tens of thousands of hours of robot data and "a couple of hours" of in-domain demonstration ([Sequoia podcast](https://sequoiacap.com/podcast/inside-the-robot-data-factory-how-mecka-sensorizes-humans-to-train-robots)). Generalist says GEN-1 needs about ten times less task-specific data than GEN-0 ([The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/generalist-introduces-gen-1-general-purpose-model-for-physical-ai/)). Dyna claims hours of local fine-tuning per platform ([Unite.AI](https://www.unite.ai/dyna-robotics-trains-dyna-2-on-a-million-hours-of-human-video-no-robot-data/)). Validation of those small datasets is done in-house and is immature. RoboForce pays model-evaluation operators $30–35 an hour ([JobGrid](https://jobgrid.eu/en/jobs/model-evaluation-operator-swing-shift-roboforce/)). Skild's post-training and deployment team builds its own evaluation pipelines ([Resumegeni](https://resumegeni.com/jobs/research-engineer-post-training-deployment-skildai-careers-cd420c83a1)). Researchers say VLA evaluation is "largely confined to single-trial success rates under curated resets" ([arXiv 2602.18813](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.18813)).

These sources point to one hypothesis. As pretraining data commoditizes, the recurring, dated, high-stakes job is to **collect a small, clean in-domain dataset for each new customer site or robot body and prove it works before go-live**. That is where the ICP's spend (the shift team) meets a deadline (the customer date). Agility is the clearest single company with both: a data-collection manager with build-versus-buy authority, and GXO sites opening in early 2027. No source links the two, so this stays an inference to test, not a finding. The strongest counterargument is that post-training and deployment teams at foundation-model companies are the better ICP, because their deadline is explicit. No source shows those teams paying outside vendors for anything other than data, though. That makes them the right fallback if OEM interviews fail the deadline test, not the starting point.

## Validate the deadline before writing a line of code

The buyer pool is small enough that validation doubles as the first sales pipeline. One founder estimates that only "a couple hundred" people worldwide approve spending on this kind of data ([Youmind repost](https://youmind.com/landing/x-viral-articles/robotics-data-broken-business)). That figure is weakly sourced, but it means every interview covers a meaningful share of the market. The target list builds itself from the job postings above. Look for people with titles such as Manager, Data Collection; Teleoperations Lead; AI Data Operations Lead; and Data Quality Manager at 1X, Agility, Foundation, Sunday, Telexistence, Mind Robotics, Watney, Nimble and Dyna, which also posts a data-collection operator role ([Rippling](https://ats.rippling.com/dyna-careers/jobs/f77f787d-5b51-43e3-877f-35c308762cd7)). Aim for 15–20 conversations with the person who owns the yield metric, not the research lead. Run them as five falsification tests:

| Hypothesis | What to ask for | Burning signal | Kill or pivot signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| The pain is yield, not volume | Current usable-episode yield, top three rejection reasons, cost per accepted episode | Manager gives a number unprompted and can name its cost | "We just need more hours": a supply problem in a crowded market |
| A deadline drives it | The last campaign that slipped, and the date it was tied to | A named training run, customer go-live or demo with a consequence | No dated consequence in most interviews: pivot to post-training teams in customer pilots |
| They already pay to fix it | Headcount, internal tools and vendors tried in the past 12 months | QA lead hired, internal tool built, or vendor tried and dropped | Nothing tried: annoyance, not burning pain |
| Vendors fail them specifically | Why they left Scale, Mecka, Micro1 or Encord, or never started | A concrete failure: spec mismatch, inconsistency, cost, data rights | "Vendor X handles this fine" |
| They will buy rather than build | Who owns build-versus-buy, and their reaction to a paid offer | Grants data access and signs a paid pilot within weeks | "We'll build it": the Figure pattern; sell to vendors' QA leads instead |

The cheapest real test is a **paid yield audit** done by hand. Take one week of a customer's episodes and their telemetry, and return a trainability score for each episode, a rejection taxonomy, operator-level feedback and a rig-fault log. The May 2026 paper's proposal for telemetry-based post-episode feedback is a ready-made method to start from ([arXiv 2605.26349](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.26349)). Charge for the audit. A manager who shares data and pays inside a month is showing the burning signal. One who praises the idea and stalls is not. In parallel, ask Scale (physical-ai@scale.com), Encord and Micro1 for minimums and pricing as a small buyer would, so the "vendors serve this badly" claim rests on documented terms rather than inference.

Several unknowns should shape the interview script. No public yield figure exists for any OEM. Most pay data comes from aggregators. Figure's vendor complaint is self-reported, and its own Index metrics count uploads, not accepted training hours ([explainx](https://explainx.ai/blog/figure-index-robot-dataset-august-2026)). No contract between a named OEM and any of the seven vendors was found. Each of these is a question to ask directly, not an assumption to build on.

## Conclusion

Value in robot data is moving from acquisition to conversion. Whoever owns the yield metric now controls the budget, and that person sits inside OEM operations, not in a vendor's sales funnel. Budget size defines this ICP as much as pain does. The buyer that hurt most (Figure) spent its way into a competing in-house system, so the reachable market is the band of companies large enough to run shifts but too small to fund a billion-dollar data build. A founder who sells "more data" walks into the most crowded corner of the market. A founder who sells measurable yield on the customer's own rigs walks into a gap the vendors are only starting to staff.

The deadline test is the decisive unknown, and the evidence suggests it shows up only when data is tied to a specific customer site. If interviews confirm that, the product to build is closer to "site-ready data", meaning in-domain collection plus validation delivered against a go-live date, than to a general collection tool. That framing also gives the founder a natural second segment (foundation-model companies in customer pilots) without a rebuild. The hiring of QA leads at Mecka and automated-QA engineers at Labelbox shows the incumbents are moving toward the same gap. The advantage goes to whoever proves yield on a customer's own rigs first, so the validation sprint matters more than the first line of product code.
